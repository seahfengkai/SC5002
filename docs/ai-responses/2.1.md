## (3) Why StandardScaler helps linear regression

**What it does:** StandardScaler rescales each feature to have **mean 0 and standard deviation 1**. Look at how different your columns are right now:

- `AveBedrms` is around **1**
- `MedInc` is around **1.7–5.7**
- `Population` is around **1,000–1,700**

After scaling, every feature is on the same scale. A value of +1 means "one standard deviation above average," whatever the feature is.

**Why that helps:**

1. **Training runs faster and more reliably.** Many models learn with *gradient descent*, which adjusts the weights step by step. When one feature is in the thousands and another is around 1, the "error landscape" becomes a long, narrow valley, and the algorithm zig-zags slowly. Scaled features make it closer to a round bowl, so it reaches the best answer faster and more stably.

2. **Regularization treats features fairly.** Ridge and Lasso regression penalise large coefficients. Without scaling, a feature's coefficient size depends on its units. `Population` needs a tiny coefficient just because its numbers are big, so the penalty barely touches it. Meanwhile `AveBedrms` needs a larger coefficient and gets penalised more. Scaling puts everyone on equal footing.

3. **Coefficients become comparable.** After scaling, each coefficient means "how much the prediction changes when this feature goes up by one standard deviation." You can then roughly compare which features matter more. Without scaling, a coefficient of 0.0001 for `Population` and 0.4 for `MedInc` can't be compared directly.

**An honest nuance:** for **plain ordinary least squares** (e.g. scikit-learn's `LinearRegression`, which solves the equations directly), scaling does **not** change the predictions or the accuracy. The model just adjusts its coefficients to compensate. Scaling pays off with gradient descent, regularization (Ridge/Lasso), and interpreting coefficients. It's still a good habit, because most real workflows involve at least one of these.

**Two practical tips:**
- **Fit the scaler on the training data only**, then use it to transform the test data. If you fit it on all the data, information from the test set "leaks" into training.
- `Latitude` and `Longitude` scale fine, but a straight-line model can't capture that *specific locations* (like the coast near San Francisco or LA) are expensive. Scaling won't fix that. It's a limitation of linear models on this dataset.
